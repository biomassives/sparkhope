For mycelial foods (aimed at risk-reduction, not full regulatory certification):

Visual, smell, and timing checks (first-line screening)
Inspect daily for unusual colors (green, black, pink, orange), sliminess, or fuzzy colonies differing from the expected white mycelium.
Smell: healthy mycelium often has a fresh, mushroomy/earthy odor; sour, putrid, or chemical smells suggest bacterial or mold contamination.
Growth speed and pattern: slow or patchy colonization can indicate contamination.
Simple plate-culture tests (low-cost, informative)
Supplies: sterile petri dishes or reusable glass dishes, general-purpose growth medium (PDA — potato dextrose agar — or nutrient agar), an inexpensive incubator (or insulated box with stable warm spot), sterile loop or swab, 70% isopropyl alcohol.
How to use:
Swab the surface of your mycelial mat (and a separate swab of air or tools) and streak onto agar plates.
Incubate at ~25–30°C for 48–72 hours.
Observe colony morphology and color. Fast-growing colored colonies (green/blue/pink/orange/black) usually signal contaminants (molds or bacteria).
Interpretation: This won’t identify species but will tell you whether potentially problematic microbes are present and give an idea of colony abundance.
Simple bacterial vs fungal discrimination
Use a selective medium or simple technique:
Add a small antibiotic (e.g., streptomycin at low concentration) to plates to suppress bacteria if you want to preferentially see fungi, or use Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) that favors fungi.
For bacteria, nutrient agar without fungicide will allow bacterial colonies to grow quickly.
A microscopic check (see 4) can also distinguish hyphae vs single-celled bacteria.
Low-cost microscopy
A basic student microscope (40–400×) lets you inspect samples:
Fungal hyphae: branching filamentous threads; septa may be visible.
Bacteria: tiny dots or rods (single cells/colonies) visible at 1000× oil immersion ideally, but 400× can still show shapes if abundant.
Prepare a wet mount or lactophenol cotton blue/iodine stain to improve contrast.
ATP bioluminescence swabs (quick hygiene check)
Portable ATP meters give a hygiene score by measuring general biological material (not species-specific).
Useful to check cleaning efficacy of workspace and equipment; a high ATP reading on harvested mats suggests poor cleanliness or heavy microbial load.
Simple rapid test strips (food safety kits)
Some lateral-flow or dipstick kits detect common pathogens/toxins (e.g., E. coli, Salmonella), though many are designed for water or meat and may need adaptation.
Use as spot checks for specific concerns, not comprehensive screening.
DIY dip-slide plates for routine monitoring
Commercial dip-slide agar tests (pre-poured agar on a strip) can be pressed against surfaces or sample suspensions, incubated in a warm place, and read against a chart. They’re inexpensive and easy for routine monitoring of total aerobic counts and yeasts/molds.
Culturing from wash/rinse and CFU estimation
Rinse a known area or weight of mycelial mat in a measured volume of sterile saline or buffered water, perform serial dilutions (10-fold), plate aliquots on nutrient agar, incubate, and count colony-forming units (CFU) to estimate microbial load per gram. This provides semi-quantitative results without advanced equipment.
Safe sample handling and record-keeping
Use gloves, clean tools, and disinfect surfaces. Label samples with date, source, and conditions. Keep a log of results to detect trends.
When to send to a lab
If you see suspicious colors/smells, plan to sell, or need regulatory/commercial certification, send samples to an accredited food microbiology lab for:
Total aerobic plate count, yeasts & molds, coliforms/E. coli, Salmonella, Listeria, and mycotoxin screens.
Labs provide definitive, species-level, and quantitative results.
Practical starter kit suggestion (low-cost)

PDA or nutrient agar pre-mixed powder and petri dishes — $20–40.
Basic student microscope (100–400×) — $50–150.
Sterile swabs, sterile saline, micropipettes or disposable droppers.
Disposable gloves, 70% isopropyl, alcohol wipes.
Dip-slide plates or ready-to-use test strips.
Optional: portable incubator or insulated box with accurate thermometer.
Limitations and safety

These low-resource methods detect many, but not all, hazards. They don’t replace certified lab testing required for commercial sale.
Do not consume products that show signs of contamination or off-odors. When in doubt, discard.
For mycotoxin concerns (some molds produce heat-stable toxins), lab testing is essential before commercial distribution.
