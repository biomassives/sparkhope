Below is a side‑by‑side comparison of the two most common ways to “feed” a liquid spawn or bulk‑substrate inoculation for Pleurotus (oyster) and Hericium (lion’s‑mane) mushrooms:

Parameter Aerated Compost Tea (ACT) – “microbial‑boost” method Sterilized‑broth / “nutrient‑only” method
Goal Add a cocktail of beneficial bacteria, fungi, humic/fulvic acids, and trace minerals that can (1) improve mycelial vigor, (2) increase resistance to contaminants, and (3) accelerate colonisation of lignocellulose. Provide a clean, high‑energy carbon source (usually glucose, malt extract, etc.) without any competing microbes.
Typical recipe (per L of water) 1. Base – 100 ml well‑aged, high‑C compost (e.g., horse‑stable, mushroom‑house spent substrate)
2. Aeration – 1 L air pump + 0.5 µm inline filter, 30 L min⁻¹ for 24–48 h
3. Additives (optional) – 1 ml kelp extract, 0.5 ml molasses, 0.2 ml humic acid solution
4. pH – 5.5–6.5 (adjust with food‑grade phosphoric acid or lime)		 1. Base – 20 g glucose (or 10 g malt extract) + 5 g yeast extract per L
2. Optional vitamins/minerals – 0.5 g MgSO₄·7H₂O, 0.1 g CaCl₂, trace‑element mix (e.g., Fe, Zn, Mn)
3. pH – 5.8–6.2 (adjust with HCl or NaOH)
Filtration / sterilisation steps • Pre‑filter through a 5 µm mesh to remove large particles.
• Fine‑filter through a 0.45 µm (or preferably 0.22 µm) membrane to eliminate most bacterial spores and fungal conidia.
• T‑0 “sterilisation” – optional brief (5 min) pasteurisation at 70 °C to kill any remaining vegetative cells while preserving the beneficial microbial community.
• Immediate use – keep the tea aerated and at 20–25 °C; use within 12 h.		 • Sterilise the entire broth in a pressure cooker/autoclave: 15 psi (121 °C) for 20 min (or 30 min for 1‑L jars).
• Cool to ≤30 °C before inoculating with sterile mycelial liquid or grain spawn.
Microbial load after preparation High – 10⁶–10⁸ CFU ml⁻¹ of beneficial bacteria (Pseudomonas, Bacillus, Actinobacteria) + trace fungal spores (Trichoderma, Chaetomium). The goal is controlled competition, not sterility. Near‑zero – <10 CFU ml⁻¹ (only the inoculated mushroom mycelium).
Typical inoculation rate 10–30 ml ACT per L of substrate‑water mix (≈1–3 % v/v). Often combined with a small amount of sterile grain spawn (5 % w/v) to guarantee the mushroom species wins the race. 5–10 ml sterile broth per L of substrate‑water mix (≈0.5–1 % v/v). No additional inoculum needed if the broth already contains a dense mycelial slurry.
Advantages • Resilience – the bacterial community can out‑compete contaminants (especially Trichoderma) and produce antibiotics, siderophores, and enzymes that break down lignin/hemicellulose.
• Improved fruiting – humic/fulvic acids act as a “stress‑buffer,” often yielding larger, faster‑pinning flushes.
• Lower energy cost – no autoclave needed for the tea itself.		 • Predictable, clean environment – eliminates the risk of unwanted microbes that could sabotage colonisation.
• Higher mycelial growth rate when the broth is rich in simple sugars.
• Easier scaling – sterile broth can be produced in bulk and stored (refrigerated) for weeks.
Disadvantages / Risks • Filtration is critical – a 0.22 µm filter is expensive and can clog quickly; any missed spores can introduce contaminants.
• Batch variability – compost composition changes with source, age, and moisture, leading to inconsistent nutrient profiles.
• Short shelf‑life – ACT must be used within hours; otherwise the beneficial microbes die off or turn pathogenic.		 • Energy‑intensive – autoclaving large volumes consumes electricity and water.
• No protective microbiome – the mushroom mycelium is left defenseless against opportunistic contaminants that may later enter the bulk substrate.
• Potential for “nutrient burn” if glucose concentration is too high (>30 g L⁻¹) for Pleurotus/Héricium, leading to osmotic stress.
Best‑fit mushroom species • Pleurotus ostreatus, P. pulmonarius – thrive on lignocellulose and benefit from bacterial lignin‑degrading enzymes.
• Hericium erinaceus – especially when grown on hardwood sawdust; the bacterial community can help solubilise lignin and release phenolics that Hericium uses.		 • All species, but especially fast‑colonising strains (e.g., P. eryngii) where you want a “clean‑room” start.
• Useful when you are working with grain spawn that will be transferred to a sterile bulk substrate later.
Typical workflow (ACT) 1. Prepare compost (2‑week aged, moisture 55 %).
2. Blend 100 ml compost + 900 ml de‑chlorinated water → coarse slurry.
3. Aerate 24 h with inline filter.
4. Fine‑filter (0.22 µm) into a sterile carboy.
5. Optional pasteurise 70 °C, 5 min → cool to 25 °C.
6. Inoculate substrate‑water mix (e.g., 1 L sawdust + 1 L water) with 10 ml ACT + 5 g sterile grain spawn.
7. Incubate 22–25 °C, 80 % RH, gentle agitation for 48 h, then static for colonisation.		 1. Mix glucose/yeast extract/minerals in de‑chlorinated water.
2. Dispense into autoclavable jars or bags (1 L each).
3. Autoclave 121 °C, 15 psi, 20 min.
4. Cool to ≤30 °C (use an ice‑water bath if needed).
5. Inoculate with 5 ml sterile mycelial slurry (or 5 g grain spawn).
6. Incubate 22–25 °C, static, until full colonisation (5–7 days for Pleurotus, 7–10 days for Hericium).

When to choose which Choose ACT when you:
– Want a “bio‑enhanced” substrate that resists Trichoderma and other fast‑growing contaminants.
– Have reliable filtration equipment and can work quickly.
– Are growing on hard‑wood sawdust or straw‑substrate where extra lignin‑degrading bacteria are beneficial.		 Choose sterile broth when you:
– Have limited filtration resources or need a reproducible, low‑variance medium.
– Are running a commercial operation where batch‑to‑batch consistency is paramount.
